You don’t have to keep it—and not keeping it doesn’t mean you’re giving up. **Newborn (neonatal) kittens are one of the most exhausting, high‑failure rescues even for experienced fosters**, and if two vets are estimating <15% survival, the kitten likely needs intensive, around‑the‑clock care. ## What’s “best” for the kitten (and for you) The kitten’s best chance is usually **getting to an experienced neonatal foster/rescue ASAP** (they may have incubators, tube-feeding skills, glucose/fluids protocols, and people rotating shifts). If you’re already exhausted now, trying to do this alone can quickly become unmanageable—and it’s kinder to transfer care early rather than after you’re completely depleted. ## What to do right now (next 1–2 hours) 1. **Get help fast** - Call/DM: local **cat rescues**, **humane society**, **municipal shelter** (ask specifically for “**neonatal kitten foster**”), and any “community cats/TNR” groups. - Ask both vets: “Do you know **any neonatal fosters or rescue partners** who can take this kitten today?” - Post in local groups (Facebook/Nextdoor): “**Neonatal orphan kitten found, needs experienced foster ASAP**” + your area. 2. **Keep the kitten warm (most critical)** - Make a small box/carrier nest with towels. - Add a **heating pad on LOW under half the box** (so it can crawl off the heat). Or a warm water bottle wrapped in cloth. - **If the kitten is cold, don’t feed yet**—warm first. Feeding a chilled kitten can be dangerous. 3. **If you must feed temporarily** - Use **kitten milk replacer (KMR)** only (no cow’s milk). - Feed with a kitten bottle/syringe **very slowly**; kitten should be **belly-down**, never on its back. - If it coughs, milk comes from nose, or breathing sounds “wet”: **stop and go to an emergency vet** (aspiration risk). 4. **Help it pee/poop** - Newborns can’t eliminate alone. After feeding, gently rub genitals/anus with a **warm damp cotton ball/tissue** until it pees/poops. ## A simple decision guide for “Should I keep it?” **Keep it only if** you can realistically do *all* of this for at least the next 1–2 weeks: - Feeding **every 2–3 hours day and night** (often more if very weak) - Constant warmth management - Stimulation to eliminate after feeds - Frequent monitoring/urgent vet visits if it declines - Emotional bandwidth for the real possibility it may still not survive If that sounds like too much (and it’s completely reasonable if it does), the most responsible choice is to **transfer to a rescue/shelter/foster** today. > The kindest thing you can do may be: **keep it warm and safe right now, then hand off to a neonatal expert as soon as possible.** ## When it’s an emergency *right now* Go to an emergency vet if you see: - Cold body, limpness, won’t latch/suckle - Gasping, constant crying then suddenly quiet/weak - Milk from nose, coughing during feeding - Severe diarrhea, bloated belly, or dehydration (sticky gums) ## Quick questions (so I can guide you to the best next step) 1.
