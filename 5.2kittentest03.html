Keeping a **few-days-old orphan kitten** is basically doing neonatal ICU at home: **heat + feeding every 2–3 hours + help peeing/pooping**, day and night, for weeks. If you’re already exhausted, it’s completely reasonable to **not** take this on yourself. You’ve already done a lot by rescuing it and getting it seen. ## What I’d do (best outcome with least burden on you) **Try to transfer the kitten to an experienced neonate foster/rescue ASAP.** Their survival odds are usually much better because they have supplies, schedules, and backup people. Contact, in this order: - **Local animal shelter / humane society** (ask specifically: “Do you have a neonatal kitten foster program?”) - **Cat rescues** in your area (call + message; many are faster on Facebook/Instagram) - **Emergency vet**: ask if they can connect you to a neonatal foster or rescue partner - If you’re in the U.S.: **Alley Cat Allies – Feral Friends Network** (free directory of local cat rescuers) If you tell me your **city/ZIP/country**, I can help you find nearby rescues/foster programs to call. ## If you keep it even short-term (hours–1 day) until a handoff These are the “musts” that make the biggest difference: ### 1) Warmth first (do this right now) A neonate can’t regulate temperature. **If it’s cold, do not feed yet** (they can’t digest and can aspirate). - Make a nest box with towels. - Heat source: heating pad on **LOW** under **half** the box (so it can crawl off heat) *or* a warm water bottle wrapped in cloth. - Aim for the nest to feel **warm, not hot**. (Overheating is also dangerous.) ### 2) Feeding (only once warm) - Use **kitten milk replacer** (KMR or equivalent). **No cow’s milk.** - Feed **belly-down** (like they’re nursing), never on their back. - Every **2–3 hours** around the clock at this age (yes, overnight). - If the kitten is weak, coughing, milk bubbling from nose, or struggling: **stop and call an emergency vet** (aspiration pneumonia risk). ### 3) Help it pee/poop After each feeding: - Gently rub genitals/anus with a **warm damp cotton ball/tissue** until it pees/poops. ### 4) Simple monitoring - If possible, weigh daily with a kitchen gram scale. Healthy gain is roughly **10–15 g/day**. - Keep it clean and dry (chilling happens fast when wet). ## When it’s not kind (or not realistic) to continue If **two vets** think survival is <15%, they may be seeing things like severe dehydration, infection, prematurity, congenital issues, hypothermia, etc. In that situation: - Handing off to a neonate-experienced rescue is the best next step. - If the kitten is clearly declining and suffering and no rescue can take it, asking a vet about **humane euthanasia** can be a compassionate option. That’s not “giving up”—it’s preventing prolonged suffering when odds are extremely low. > **Takeaway:** You don’t “owe” it raising it yourself. The kindest move may be getting it to a neonatal foster/rescue quickly—or making a humane decision with veterinary guidance. ## Red flags = emergency now Go to an emergency vet immediately if you see: - Trouble breathing, gasping, blue/gray gums/tongue - Extreme lethargy, limp body, won’t latch at all - Continuous crying that doesn’t settle when warm - Vomiting, severe diarrhea, bloated hard belly - Cold body temperature that won’t warm up If you want, tell me: 1) Are its **eyes still closed**? 2) Does it feel **warm or cool** to the touch right now? 3) What did the vets say is wrong (dehydration, infection, low temp, etc.)? 4) Your rough location (city/ZIP/country) …and I’ll help you choose the most realistic next step and find rescue contacts nearby.
